Knowledge

About HIL Simulation

While preparing the test strategy for a complex, safety-critical ECU, I use a HIL facility from the earliest project stages — verifying generated code on an evaluation board, then RCP, then target hardware, following the V-model through MIL, SIL, PIL and HIL.

HIL Across the V-Cycle

HIL is a versatile tool that extends far beyond integration and testing — it applies across the entire V-cycle, from early proof-of-concept to deployment and maintenance.

Early research & proof of concept: test the feasibility of approaches in a controlled environment before committing to a design.

Requirements: validate and refine specifications by simulating the operational environment.

Architecture & design: assess how components interact in real time to inform structure and interfaces.

Implementation & integration: test components in isolation, then combine real hardware with simulated environments.

Test & validation: rigorous, repeatable testing including edge cases too risky for the real world.

Deployment & maintenance: ongoing regression testing as updates and patches are applied.

Signal, Power & Mechanical HIL

HILS can be implemented at three levels, each suited to a different kind of system interaction.

Signal Level HIL: the most common form — the ECU's control units interact with the simulated environment via digital or analog electrical signals.

Power Level HIL: simulates the flow of power itself — real-world electrical loads, battery systems, motors, or entire power grids.

Mechanical Level HIL: simulates mechanical loads and interactions, often via physical actuators or hydraulic systems that mimic real-world forces and motion.

Coupling HILS with RCP

Rapid Control Prototyping lets engineers deploy Simulink-designed control algorithms straight onto target hardware — pairing it with HILS makes for a fast, low-risk validation workflow.

The development board runs the actual control software while HILS simulates the rest of the system (vehicle, plant, or mechanical system) — enabling thorough testing without the cost or risk of full real-world trials.

Benefits: iterative development and validation, early risk reduction, and cost efficiency versus physical prototypes.

Applications: automotive ECU software validated against a simulated vehicle; aerospace flight-control systems tested against simulated aircraft dynamics; robotic controllers validated against a simulated environment before hardware is at risk.
